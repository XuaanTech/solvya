## Why write numbers in words?

Anyone can read the figure "1,250.00", but far fewer people can write that amount correctly in words. On **checks**, **invoices** and **contracts**, writing the amount literally is not a bank's whim — it is a **security measure**. If the amount appears only as digits, a small alteration — adding a zero, changing a digit — could change the value with nobody noticing. The written-out version fixes the amount both parties agreed on.

That is why banks require checks to show the amount in both figures and words: when the two disagree, the written words usually prevail.

## What is numbers-to-words conversion for?

Converting numbers to words turns up in more situations than you might expect:

- **Bank checks**: writing the amount to prevent fraud and tampering.
- **Invoices**: stating the amount in words when a customer requests it or the law requires it.
- **Contracts and agreements**: recording payment amounts unambiguously in economic clauses.
- **Notarial documents**: deeds, powers of attorney and minutes often include literal amounts.
- **Accounting**: verifying entries and reconciling figures in financial reports.

In every one of these cases, a spelling mistake in the conversion can invalidate the document. An automatic converter removes that risk.

## How the conversion works

Converting a number to words follows **grouping by place value**. Every digit belongs to a unit, tens, hundreds, thousands, and so on. The process combines three basic ideas:

1. **Tens and units combine with "and"**: 23 → "twenty-three", 45 → "forty-five".
2. **Hundreds connect with a following number**: 100 → "one hundred", but 150 → "one hundred and fifty".
3. **Decimals are read as cents**: 123.45 → "one hundred and twenty-three and forty-five cents".

> **Tip**: In English, use a hyphen for compound numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine, but never between the hundreds and the tens.

## Differences between Spanish and English

Although the principle is the same, the two languages have important nuances:

- **The English "and"**: English inserts "and" between hundreds and tens or units (one hundred **and** fifty). Spanish does not.
- **"Cien" vs "ciento"**: Spanish distinguishes "cien" (100) from "ciento" (100 + something); English always uses "hundred".
- **Decimals**: English uses a decimal point and the word "cents"; Spanish uses a comma and "céntimos".
- **Compound numbers**: Spanish joins "veintiuno", "veintidós"; English writes them with a hyphen (twenty-one, thirty-two).
